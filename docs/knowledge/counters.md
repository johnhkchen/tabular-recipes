# Counters

**A counter is where you would get this if you were not making it at home.** Not a cuisine, not
a category — a kind of shop or window a person recognises from their own street. Each counter's
page on this site is a menu, and every item on it opens a recipe instead of going into a cart.

Two things follow from that, and they are the reason this file exists.

1. **The counters have to be archetypes, not taxonomies.** They were settled from six passes of
   real menu reading (roughly seventy menus opened end to end, several hundred listings and
   guides skimmed) rather than from the recipes already written. Where the menus showed that two
   formats genuinely combine, they are one counter here. Where the menus showed them separate,
   they are separate. Each entry says which, and why.
2. **The menu word is the way in.** Someone who ate a "#1 combo bánh mì" at the place down the
   street and wants to recreate the pâté does not know to search for "pork liver pâté". They
   know what the board said. So every counter below carries a vocabulary table, and **that table
   is the source for `>> aka:` lines.** Be generous with it. Spelling on real boards drifts hard
   — hommus/hommos/hummus, wat/wot/wet, shawarma/shwarma/shawerma, revuelta/mixta — and the
   variants sat on menus in the same city, so they are all real names and all worth recording.

The fallback in `src/data/counters.json` (`categories`) only catches recipes that name no counter
at all. It is coarse on purpose. Prefer a `>> counters:` line.

## Bakery

**What it is.** The corner bakery: a rack of plain loaves and rolls on one side, a glass case of
sweet things on the other, most of it sold by the piece rather than made to order. Bread and
sweets are printed as two separate lists on nearly every board — a Mexican shop heads them "Pan
Dulce" and "Pan Salado", a Hong Kong shop separates the bun case from the cake counter, a
Japanese shop puts sweet buns and savoury buns in different sections beside a shokupan loaf.

**Why it is one counter and not five.** Every researcher found a bakery-shaped counter, and they
found the same shape in five traditions — Hong Kong bun shop, Japanese bread case, Arabic bakery
with a dip case, Brazilian padaria, and the babka-and-rugelach end of a Jewish appetizing
counter. The vocabulary differs; the shop does not. The one bakery that earns its own counter is
the [Panadería](#panaderia), because there the service model itself is different.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Pineapple Bun with Butter | bo lo yau, 菠蘿油 | The same bun split warm with a cold slab of butter laid inside so it half melts. |

## Panadería

**What it is.** A Mexican bakery, and the format is self-serve rather than ordered: you take a
metal tray and a pair of tongs at the door, fill it from open racks, and hand it over at the
register. **Nothing is numbered and nothing is described**, which is exactly why the item names
are the only vocabulary a customer ever learns — the shelf is the menu. Hours skew early, often
from 5am, and several are cash only. One long "Pan Dulce" heading (one shop listed about
twenty-eight items under it) plus a separate "Pan Salado" rack holding bolillo, telera and pan de
torta; then cakes, flan and tamales from the same counter.

**Separate, not folded into Bakery.** Two independent passes found the same named items recurring
between shops several blocks apart — conchas, orejas, cuernos and fruit empanadas on every single
list; marranitos, campechanas, mantecadas and roles de canela on most — which is what makes them
a shared vocabulary rather than one bakery's list.

| On the menu | Also called | Plainly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concha | conchas, pan de huevo, shell bread, bomba, pan dulce | A soft, slightly sweet enriched bun with a paste of sugar, fat and flour pressed on top and scored into a shell pattern. Vanilla, chocolate or pink strawberry. |
| Bolillo | bolillito, pan francés, Mexican roll | A crusty white oval roll, soft inside, split down the top. The savoury bread of the shop — this is what a torta is built on. |
| Telera | pan telera | Flatter and softer than a bolillo, with two creases pressed down its back so it tears into three. The other torta roll. |
| Oreja | orejas, ears, palmier, elephant ear | Puff pastry rolled in sugar, folded, sliced and flattened so it bakes out into a crisp caramelised ear. |
| Puerquito | marranito, cochinito, cochito, pig cookie, gingerbread pig, cochinito de piloncillo | A soft, cakey molasses and piloncillo cookie cut in the shape of a pig. Tastes of unrefined cane sugar and cinnamon more than of ginger. |
| Campechana | — | A wide sheet of flaky pastry glazed with sugar until it shatters when you pick it up. |
| Cuerno | cuernito, cuernitos, Mexican croissant, horn | A crescent roll — softer, sweeter and more bread-like than a French croissant, sometimes glazed. |
| Bigote | bigote de piña, bigotes, mustache | A long filled roll curled up at both ends like a moustache, usually pineapple or a light cream inside. |
| Polvorón | polvorón rosa, polvorones, pink cookie | A crumbly shortbread that goes to powder in your mouth, tinted pink, yellow or brown. |
| Cubilete | cubilete de queso, cheese cup, cheese tart | A small sweet cheese custard baked in a paper cup, somewhere between a tartlet and a cheesecake. |
| Mantecada | mantecadas, panqué, Mexican muffin | A dense buttery pound-cake muffin baked in a fluted paper case, plain and eggy. |
| Empanada de Piña | empanada de calabaza, empanada de camote, fruit empanada | A folded turnover of sweet dough around pineapple, pumpkin or sweet potato. |
| Tres Leches | pastel de tres leches, three milk cake | A plain sponge soaked through with evaporated milk, condensed milk and cream, topped with whipped cream. |
| Pan de Muerto | pan de los muertos, bread of the dead | A round orange-blossom-scented sweet bread with strips of dough laid across the top like bones, dusted heavily in sugar. Autumn only. |
| Pan Salado | pan blanco | The unsweetened side of the bakery — the rolls and loaves, listed away from the sweet case. |
| Flan Napolitano | flan, chocoflan | Baked egg custard turned out under its own caramel. Chocoflan is the one baked in the same tin as a chocolate cake so they swap places. |

**Also on the same counter, when the shop is also a tortillería:** masa fresca (wet nixtamalised
corn dough by the pound, ground that morning), masa preparada (already beaten with fat and salt,
ready to spread on husks for tamales), tortillas by the kilo and by the pound, tortilla taquera
(the small four-inch street-taco size), totopos (chips), tostadas, chicharrón, crema mexicana
(thick soured cream, looser and less sour than American sour cream), queso fresco (soft, salty,
crumbly, does not melt).

## Taquería

**What it is.** An order-at-the-register counter with a griddle and usually a vertical spit. **The
menu is a grid, not a list**: a fixed set of filling words gets applied across a fixed set of
vehicles, and the customer names the pair. Real taquería boards are *not* numbered — the size
ladder does the work instead: Regular / Deluxe / Super, with Petite and Mondo at the extremes.
Numbered combination plates ("No. 1", "No. 2", running into the teens) belong to the older
sit-down Tex-Mex format, which is a different place.

**Separate.** The taco counter and the numbered-plate restaurant were never the same board in any
menu read. The Salvadoran pupusería is also its own format — a griddle counter whose fillings
list is the menu, with curtido and salsa roja arriving unpriced alongside — but its boards
routinely also carry Honduran baleadas and Mexican tacos, so its vocabulary is recorded at the
bottom of this entry rather than given a counter of its own.

| On the menu | Also called | Plainly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Super Burrito | Burrito Super, super, Deluxe, Regular | The same burrito with more added, not a bigger tortilla. "Regular" is meat, rice, beans, salsa; "Super" adds cheese, sour cream and guacamole. Ordering by the size word is how people order here. |
| Al Pastor | pastor, adobada, trompo | Pork marinated red with dried chile and achiote, stacked on a vertical spit and shaved off as the outside chars, usually with a bit of pineapple. |
| Carnitas | roast pork | Pork shoulder cooked slowly submerged in its own fat, then pulled apart and crisped. |
| Lengua | beef tongue | Whole beef tongue simmered until soft, peeled, then diced and crisped on the griddle. Mild and very tender — more like pot roast than offal. |
| Suadero | res | A thin beef cut from between the belly and the leg, cooked slowly in its own fat until it pulls apart. |
| Cachete | beef cheek, cabeza | Braised beef cheek — the softest, fattiest thing on the board. |
| Tripa | tripas, tripe | Small intestine boiled then crisped hard on the griddle, chewy and crunchy at once. |
| Chile Verde Pork | chile verde, puerco en salsa verde | Pork stewed until falling apart in a sauce of tomatillos and green chiles. |
| Quesabirria | birria taco, taco de birria, red taco, quesa birria | Chile-braised shredded beef and melted cheese folded into a tortilla dipped in the braising fat and griddled red, with a cup of broth to dip it in. |
| Consomé | consome, dipping broth, caldo | The strained broth the birria cooked in, served in a cup for dipping. |
| Quesadilla Suiza | suiza, Swiss-style quesadilla | A quesadilla made deliberately rich — extra melting cheese and sometimes cream. "Suiza" is menu shorthand for creamy, not for Swiss cheese. |
| Chile Relleno | relleno | A whole roasted poblano stuffed with cheese, dipped in whipped egg batter and fried. Often sold as its own burrito filling. |
| Mojado | wet burrito, enchilada-style, with red sauce | A finished burrito flooded with red chile sauce and melted cheese, eaten with a fork off a plate. |
| Mulita | mulitas | Two small tortillas with meat and cheese between them — a little sandwich rather than a folded quesadilla. |
| Vampiro | vampiros, vampire taco | A tortilla griddled flat and hard like a cracker, cheese melted on top, then the meat. |

| Huarache | huaraches, super huarache | A long oval of masa shaped like a sandal sole, fried, spread with beans, then piled with toppings. |
| Sope | sopes, picadita, garnacha | A thick little masa round with a pinched-up rim, fried, holding beans and toppings inside the wall. |
| Alambre | alambres, alambre de res | Meat chopped on the griddle with bacon, peppers, onion and cheese, scooped into tortillas at the table. |
| Torta | torta cubana, pambazo, lonche, Mexican sandwich | The same fillings on a bolillo roll with beans smeared inside. The cubana piles on everything; the pambazo is dipped in chile sauce before griddling. |
| Flauta | flautas, taquito, rolled taco | A tortilla rolled tight around filling and deep-fried into a flute. |
| Machaca | machaca con huevo | Dried, shredded beef rehydrated and scrambled into eggs. A breakfast item. |
| Salsa Roja / Salsa Verde | roja, verde, table salsa | The two table sauces — cooked chile with tomato, and cooked chile with tomatillo. |
| Agua Fresca | aguas frescas, horchata, jamaica, tamarindo | A thin sweet drink — rice and cinnamon, hibiscus, or fruit — sold by the cup from a jar. |

**Pupusería vocabulary, on the same kind of block.** Pupusa Revuelta (also *Mixta* — the two
spellings appeared on two full boards; a thick hand-patted corn cake stuffed before griddling
with pork, beans and cheese together, the default one); Queso con Loroco (cheese with loroco, a
green flower bud tasting between artichoke and cut grass); Chicharrón (here it means pork cooked
down and ground to a soft moist paste, **not** crackling); Curtido (lightly pickled shredded
cabbage with carrot and oregano, sour and crunchy, the whole point of eating it with something
heavy); Pupusa Loca (the everything one, made larger); Yuca Frita con Chicharrón; Pastelito (a
small fried masa turnover of meat and potato); Baleada (Honduran — a thick soft flour tortilla
folded over refried beans, cream and cheese); Crema Salvadoreña (thinner and tangier than
Mexican crema); Sopa de Gallina; Pinto (beans and rice cooked together, also *casamiento*).

## Dim Sum Counter

**What it is.** The Cantonese daytime counter: steamed and fried dim sum priced by the piece
(almost always marked "(3)" to an order), a bakery case at the till, and in many shops cooked wok
food sold by the pound out of trays. Combos are not numbered — they are stated as a rule, which
is exactly how customers say them: *"any 3 items with white rice"*, *"any 2 items with fried rice
or chow mein."* A shorthand recurs across guides for the four everyone orders: the "Four Heavenly
Kings" — har gow, siu mai, char siu bao, dan tat.

**Combined, and contested — read this before reclassifying.** In dense Chinatowns the dim sum
hall (sit-down, carts or a tick-box sheet, daytime hours), the bun shop and the roast-meat window
are three storefronts. In San Francisco one small storefront was found selling all three at once,
and a Chicago business is literally named "Bakery & Dim Sum". They are one counter here because
the vocabulary is one vocabulary and the shopper's experience is one errand; the *bakery* side of
it also lives at [Bakery](#bakery), and a recipe can name both. **Roast meats are folded in here**
— the window with ducks hanging in the glass is a real separate shop, and if this collection ever
carries enough of it (char siu, siu yuk, soy sauce chicken) it should be split back out.

| On the menu | Also called | Plainly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Har Gow | har gao, ha gow, haa gaau, xia jiao, 蝦餃, shrimp dumpling, crystal shrimp dumpling | Whole shrimp in a pleated wheat-starch skin that steams translucent, so you can see the pink through it. The pleat count is how people judge the kitchen. |
| Siu Mai | shu mai, shumai, siu maai, shao mai, siomai, 燒賣 | An open-topped dumpling of coarsely chopped pork and shrimp in a thin yellow wheat wrapper, usually with a dot of orange roe. |

| Lo Mai Gai | no mai gai, nor mai gai, nuo mi ji, 糯米雞, sticky rice in lotus leaf | Glutinous rice steamed inside a lotus leaf around chicken, sausage and mushroom, so the rice takes the leaf's tea-like smell. |

## Takeout Counter

**What it is.** The Chinese-American takeout menu — **the most standardised board of any counter
here.** Two full menus from opposite ends of the country ran near-identical sections in
near-identical order: Appetizer, Soup, Chow Mein or Chop Suey, Lo Mein, Egg Foo Young,
Vegetables, Fried Rice, then Pork / Beef / Seafood / Chicken, then Mei Fun, Diet Menu, House
Specialties, Combination Platters, Lunch Special, Side Orders.

**Numbering is the customer's handle, and it is a scheme rather than a mapping.** `C1`–`C16` for
combination platters and `L1`–`L16` for lunch specials appeared on separate restaurants with
*different dishes at the same numbers* — General Tso's was C16/L1 at one place and C12 at
another. Combos come "served with pork fried rice and an egg roll". Chinese characters print
beside the English. Do not treat a number as an identifier.

**Separate from the [Dim Sum Counter](#dim-sum-counter).** Different room, different hours,
different vocabulary; the overlap is only char siu.

## Phở & Bánh Mì

**What it is.** One small Vietnamese kitchen selling soup, sandwiches and rice plates, usually
with a cold case of pork loaves, sticky rice and chè beside the register. **This is the format
where numbering matters most.** One board numbered items 1 through 33 straight through, across
appetisers, phở, rice plates and vermicelli, so customers say "14" or "number 23" rather than a
dish name. Others use letter-plus-number codes by section — `P` for phở, `A` for appetisers, `B`
for bún, `C` for cơm, `S` for sandwiches. Of seven bánh mì boards read, five numbered their
sandwiches and on four of those five **Đặc Biệt was item 1**, so "the number one" and "dac biet"
usually mean the same order. Sandwich shops print the Vietnamese name and the English gloss side
by side, which is the single most useful vocabulary bridge found anywhere.

**Combined, and contested.** Two of three passes treated the phở house and the bánh mì window as
separate formats with different menu grammars — and they are, at the extremes. But one San
Francisco pass found them reliably in one storefront, and one of three phở houses sold bánh mì
from the same kitchen under its own code block. They are one counter because a cook who wants the
pâté, the pickles, the pork loaf and the broth is shopping at one place.

## Ramen Shop

**What it is.** A counter that sells one bowl at a time. **Ramen menus are organised unlike any
other format here: the top-level split is the broth, not the protein** — "Tonkotsu Ramen" gets
its own section with six variations inside it. Then a toppings list you tick off (often bundled as
Toppings A/B/C rather than sold singly), a short fried-snack list, and a donburi section. Combos
are lettered rather than numbered — Combo A, B, C, D — so people order "the B".

**Separate from sushi.** Of nineteen ramen listings in one city guide, not one was also described
as a sushi bar; two full ramen menus had zero sushi but both had gyoza, karaage and rice bowls.
Where the formats *do* combine is the suburban everything-Japanese menu — rolls, hibachi, bento
and poke on one document — and that is a different place, not this one. Poke and rice-bowl shops
likewise run their own build-your-own grid (base → protein → mix-ins → sauce → toppings).

## Curry House

**What it is.** The neighbourhood Indian restaurant and takeaway, and **the menu structure is the
giveaway: the curry section is a list of *sauces*, priced across every protein.** "Bhuna" is a
row; chicken, lamb, prawn and king prawn are the columns — so "chicken korma" and "lamb korma"
are two printed lines rather than a choice. Sections by protein (Chicken / Boneless Lamb /
Seafood / Vegetarian) repeat the same six or eight sauces inside each. Naan gets its own section
with six or more variants. UK boards add a papadom-and-chutney-tray opening; US boards near San
Francisco commonly print a pizza section using their own tikka masala sauce.

**Spelling drifts hard here, so be generous with `aka`:** Massala/Masala, Passanda/Pasanda,
Sheekh/Seekh, Bindi/Bhindi, Papadom/Poppadom/Papadum, Daal/Dal/Dhal.

**The South Indian tiffin counter is folded in here.** It is genuinely a different shop — a dosa
grid rather than a sauce list, crossing how the batter is cooked (Plain, Butter, Mysore, Rava,
Paper, Ghee Roast, Set) against whether the potato goes in — but only one pass read one of its
menus, so it does not yet stand alone. Its vocabulary is at the bottom of this entry. If enough
dosa, idli, vada and sambar recipes accumulate, split it out as **Dosa Counter**.

## Thai Kitchen

**What it is.** A Thai restaurant or takeaway, one of the most evenly spread formats in a city
rather than clustered in one neighbourhood. The conventions are specific and repeatable: a "Lunch
Special" section where every line is prefixed `L-`, a separate "Special COMBO" pairing one entrée
with fried rice or an egg roll, **curries listed by colour** (green, yellow, red, panang), and a
spice level given as a number out of five. Most items print a transliteration and an English
gloss joined on one line — "Gaeng Keow Wan – Green curry", "Pad Kee Mao – Drunken Noodle" —
and **neither half is optional, because a customer might remember either.**

**Separate.** The local marker worth keeping: SF Thai kitchens print garlic noodles — a San
Francisco invention out of Vietnamese kitchens — right next to pad thai.

## Shawarma Counter

**What it is.** The most algorithmic board of any counter here, and it repeats shop to shop: **pick
one of three or four proteins** (chicken shawarma, beef-lamb shawarma or gyro, falafel, or the
combo of two), **then pick a format** (over rice, in a wrap or pita, as a platter, or loaded
fries), **and it arrives with a white sauce and a red sauce.** Sides are the mezze list in
miniature — hummus, baba, tabbouleh, dolma, batata, labneh, lentil soup. Numbering is real here:
one board's items are literally titled "1. Chicken over Rice".

**Combined, deliberately.** Four fine-grained variants of one grid were found separately — the
halal cart, the Levantine shawarma-and-falafel shop, the Greek gyro window, and the sit-down
mezze room — and they are the same spit, the same formats and largely the same sides list, with
different sauce words. They are one counter. Two things to keep in mind when classifying:
**mezze is a form, not a place** (a bean or chickpea dip, a smoked eggplant dip, a herb salad, a
bread salad, a wrapped leaf, a fried pastry, a fried cheese, a sausage — filled with Levantine,
Turkish, Greek or Moroccan words), and **Greek boards keep their own dip vocabulary** rather than
borrowing the Levantine one, so both sets are recorded below. If enough Greek recipes accumulate
(avgolemono, spanakopita, saganaki, loukoumades), split out a **Gyro Shop**.

**Transliteration is least stable here of anywhere.** hommus/hommos/hummus/hummous,
shawarma/shwarma/shawerma, tawook/tawouk/taouk, kafta/kefta/kufta, kibbeh/kibbie/kibbi,
manakish/manaeesh/man'oushe, tabbouleh/tabouli, labneh/labaneh — all seen on boards in the same
city. Record them all.

## Pizzeria

## Deli

**What it is.** The counter where things are sliced, spread and sold by weight, and where the
bread question comes before the filling question. Four related shops are one counter here, and
the trade actually distinguishes them: a **delicatessen** sells cured meat, hot, on rye; an
**appetizing store** sells fish and dairy and no meat, which is the exact mirror image; an
Italian **salumeria** or pork store sells cold cuts, cheese and a hot tray case; a Polish or
German **sausage counter** sorts its case by cut and cure rather than by dish. The
**sandwich shop** end of it names sandwiches rather than numbering them — one Philadelphia board
carried about forty with house names and not one number — and its identifying list is the
*bread* list, not the meat list.

**Combined, on purpose.** Everyone calls all of them "the deli". If enough recipes accumulate on
any one side — a wall of kiełbasa, or a real smoked-fish counter — split it back out.

## Smokehouse

**What it is.** The pit. Meat is sold by weight and by cut, and **the vocabulary splits sharply by
region — that split is the whole story.** One Tennessee menu sold pulled pork, sliced brisket,
burnt ends, St. Louis cut ribs and smoked bologna. One North Carolina menu sold no beef at all,
never used the word "pulled", and offered exactly three cuts of pork — Chopped, Coarse Chopped
and Sliced — organised by Plate versus Tray rather than by meat. Banana pudding was on both
dessert lists.

**Separate.** Nothing about this board is the diner's or the meat-and-three's, except that all
three sell banana pudding.

## Meat and Three

**What it is.** The cafeteria line: **you pick one cooked meat from a list that rotates by the day
and three things from the vegetable list, and cornbread comes whether you ask or not.** Some
boards print the phrase as the section header; others print "entree + 3 sides" and everyone calls
it the same thing. One thing to know before ordering: **on these menus "vegetable" means "anything
that isn't the meat"** — macaroni and cheese, candied yams, cornbread dressing and steamed rice
are all on the vegetable list, so it is easy to order three starches by accident.

## Cha Chaan Teng

**What it is.** The Hong Kong tea café, and the thing that makes it a counter rather than a
restaurant is that **its menu is a grid of times of day, not a list of dishes**. 早餐 breakfast,
常餐 the constant set that runs all day and is named for exactly that, 快餐 the fast set that only
exists over lunch and comes out already cooked, 下午茶餐 the afternoon tea set, which is where the
fried food lives. **The drink is in the price**: one Sydney board prints its whole set block as
"Each includes Milk Tea or Coffee", and the Hong Kong convention is that the drink is hot unless you
pay about $2 to $3 more to take it cold, and the bread is untoasted unless you pay about $5 to have
it grilled. The trading day is long — roughly 5 or 6am to 1am — and split into five named services:
早市, 午市, 下午茶, 晚市, 宵夜. The three big chains all print the afternoon tea window as 2pm to
5pm; the general Hong Kong afternoon-tea hours are 2:30 to 5:30.

The kitchen behind it is a griddle, a deep-fryer, a toaster, a pot of boiling water for macaroni and
instant noodles, and a tea urn — which is why the food is **Western food re-made in Hong Kong**.
Macaroni in ham broth, a pork chop baked under tomato sauce and cheese, spaghetti in the same red
sauce, a club sandwich, borscht, French toast, an omelette. Reading three boards in three cities
(Tsim Sha Tsui, Glebe in Sydney, and Sunset Park in Brooklyn) they printed the same five blocks in
the same order: a toast-and-sandwich block, a rice-plate block, a noodles-in-soup block, a long
drinks list, and the sets. The Brooklyn board is in English only and is the best evidence of what a
reader will actually search: *Borsch Soup*, *Butter & Condensed Milk with Toast*, *Spam & Egg
Sandwich*, *Satay Beef Sandwich*, *Coffee & Tea*, *Baked Pork Chop Over Rice / Spaghetti*.

**Separate, and it is not close.** The comparison is with the two Chinese counters already here,
and each fails on a different axis.

Against the [Dim Sum Counter](#dim-sum-counter): that board states its combo as a rule you say out
loud — *"any 3 items with white rice"* — and what is in the price is rice. This board states its
combo as a time of day with a fixed shape, and what is in the price is a drink. Dim sum runs late
morning to mid-afternoon; a cha chaan teng opens before six and closes after midnight. Dim sum is
steamer and fryer; this is griddle, fryer, toaster and pasta pot. And the food is not the same food:
nothing on a dim sum trolley is macaroni.

Against the [Takeout Counter](#takeout-counter): that board's handle is a number, `C1`–`C16` and
`L1`–`L16`, and the same number means a different dish at a different shop. This board's handle is
a word — 常餐, 快餐 — and it means the same thing everywhere, because it names *when* rather than
*what*. The takeout combo comes with pork fried rice and an egg roll; the cha chaan teng set comes
with milk tea. The takeout board is Chinese-American and its centre of gravity is a brown sauce; the
cha chaan teng board is Hong Kong-British and its centre of gravity is evaporated milk.

**The overlaps are real and are not a problem.** 蛋撻 is sold at a bakery, off a dim sum trolley and
over this counter, and 菠蘿包 at a bakery and here — both are already shelved at two counters each in
this collection, which is the collection agreeing with the boards. 乾炒牛河 appears on the dim sum
noodle line and on this one. 叉燒 turns up at all three. A recipe on two boards is normal; `counters`
is a list for this reason.

**The 冰室 is folded in.** A bing sutt is the older, narrower shop — drinks, toast, buns and ice
desserts, no full rice plates — and at the extremes it is a different storefront. But every board
read prints both vocabularies together: the Tsim Sha Tsui shop calls itself 冰室 and sells 湯飯,
煲仔飯 and a curry section. One counter, and the bing sutt words are in the table below.

## What could not be verified

Read this before treating anything above as settled.

**There is no census of counters.** Not one pass found a count of taquerías as against Mexican
restaurants generally, of slice shops as against sit-down pizzerias, of panaderías, Chinese
bakeries, pupuserías, delis or boba shops. Every "this repeated on every menu I opened" means
exactly that and no more, over samples of five to fifteen menus chosen by searching. The samples
are biased twice: toward shops that publish a menu at all (a tortillería that only sells
tortillas often has none), and toward whichever region those shops cluster in online — New York
metro for salumeria and appetizing, Texas and California for panadería and tortillería, Chicago
for the Polish smokehouse, shipping sites rather than neighbourhood counters for Indian mithai.

**Numbering is real for some counters and a dead end for others.** Confirmed on actual boards:
`C1`–`C16` and `L1`–`L16` at Chinese takeout (with *different dishes at the same numbers* between
restaurants, so a number is not an identifier); `B1`–`B6` bento and `D2`–`D14` sushi entrées on a
combined menu; lettered ramen combos and bundled Toppings A/B/C; `L-` prefixed Thai lunch
specials; a Vietnamese board numbered 1–33 straight through every section; five of seven bánh mì
boards numbered with Đặc Biệt at #1 on four of those five; letter-plus-number Vietnamese section
codes (P/A/B/C/S) on all three phở menus read; a halal item literally titled "1. Chicken over
Rice"; numbered Salvadoran breakfast plates; numbered stuffed pizzas; numbered Brazilian snack
combos; "Meal #1"–"Meal #7" at a Chicago fish-and-chicken counter. **Not found:** any numbered
taquería board at all (they use the Regular/Deluxe/Super size ladder instead), any numbering on
Greek, Levantine, Peruvian or Ethiopian boards, any numbering at a hoagie shop (about forty
sandwiches, all named, none numbered), and — the one most wanted — a bánh mì board actually
showing "#1 combo with pâté" as printed. That phrase is attested in a city guide, not on a board
anyone could open.

**Names one step removed from the label.** Korean banchan-case names came from online-store
product listings ("Jinmichae-bokkeum – Spicy Stir-Fried Dried Squid Strips"), never from a
photographed tub. Middle Eastern dip cases are described in aggregate on store pages ("dozens of
dips daily", "over a dozen flavors of hummus") rather than itemised. Panadería pan dulce names
come from neighbourhood articles and pan-dulce guides as much as from any shop's own list,
because panaderías mostly do not publish item lists — the shelf is the menu.

**Romanisations supplied rather than transcribed.** The dim sum menus read printed English plus
Chinese characters, not romanisation, so har gow, siu mai and cha siu bao are as written on a
board but lo bak go, dan tat, lo mai gai, cheung fun, jin deui, ma lai gao and bo lo bao are
standard usage supplied here. Looser phonetic spellings seen in one guide (Fong Djau, Djeen Dui,
Nor Mai Gai, Lohr Bahk Go, Hom Sui Gok) are that guide's, not a survey of boards. The ramen egg
is written "1/2 Egg" on the menu read; "ajitama" is a gloss. Cantonese pajeori, kongnamul and oi
muchim have no menu-as-written source, because banchan is almost never itemised — which is itself
the finding.

**Spelling decisions still open**, recorded both ways rather than picked: Revuelta vs Mixta for
the mixed pupusa (one full board each way); Esfirra vs Sfiha (one Brazilian board printed both,
in different sections, and one line printed "Kibe - Quibe"); wat/wot/wet and
beyaynetu/beyainatu; Massala/Masala; tawook/tawouk/taouk; kafta/kefta/kufta; tabbouleh/tabouli;
hummus/hommos; baba ghanoush/babaghanouj/moutabal. The `>> aka:` field is where all of these
belong, and it should be generous.

**Units nobody could pin down.** Whether panadería pan dulce is priced per piece or by weight —
one guide says roughly a dollar each, another says the tray is weighed, and both appear true at
different shops. Whether Cantonese roast meats are commonly sold by the pound: exactly one
by-the-pound siu yuk listing was found, against many priced by the half or whole bird. Whether a
North Carolina barbecue "plate" or "tray" holds more meat: it varies by pit and the menus do not
say.

**Archetypes found and deliberately not shelved.** Each of these is real, was seen in menus, and
lost only on slot count or on evidence thinness. If recipes accumulate, they are the first
candidates to split out.

  menu; a grid, not a list; genuinely nothing like the sauce-list-by-protein board.
- **Roast Meat Window** (Cantonese siu mei) — folded into
  [Dim Sum Counter](#dim-sum-counter). A separate storefront in every dense Chinatown.
- **Bun Shop** (Hong Kong bakery) and **Bread Case** (Japanese bakery) — folded into
  [Bakery](#bakery) and [Dim Sum Counter](#dim-sum-counter).
- **Gyro Shop** (Greek) — folded into [Shawarma Counter](#shawarma-counter), against the finding
  that Greek boards keep their own dip vocabulary rather than borrowing the Levantine one.
- **Pupusería** (Salvadoran) — vocabulary recorded under [Taquería](#taqueria). Two verbatim
  menus, and its boards carry Honduran and Mexican items too.
- **Clam Shack** (New England fried seafood) — two verbatim menus, and the whole-belly-clam vs
  clam-strip distinction is the most load-bearing thing on that board. Dropped for coverage, not

- **Creole Kitchen** and the boudin-and-cracklin **meat market** — vocabulary recorded under

- **Fish and Chicken** (plexiglass window, jack salmon, mild sauce, numbered meals), **Tofu

**Two things nobody looked at**, so do not trust them if they appear anywhere: the cheese
shop/creamery (the shops exist but none published a counter list with names and units), and the
standalone fishmonger and olive bar.

**No prices are recorded anywhere in this file, on purpose.** Where a search result quoted one it
was ignored: single-outlet, undated, and not what a recipe needs.
